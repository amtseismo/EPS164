# Far-Field Earthquake Radiation

Last lecture we showed that earthquakes radiate seismic energy directionally.

For a double-couple source, the far-field P-wave displacement has the form:

$$
u_i^P(\mathbf{x},t)
=
\frac{1}{4\pi \rho \alpha^3}
\frac{1}{r}
R_i^P(\theta,\phi)
\,\dot{M}\left(t-\frac{r}{\alpha}\right)
$$

where:
- $\rho$ = density
- $\alpha$ = P-wave velocity
- $r$ = source-receiver distance
- $R_i^P$ = radiation pattern
- $\dot{M}(t)$ = time derivative of seismic moment

This equation contains several physically important pieces:

1. **Geometric spreading:** $1/r$ Wave amplitudes decrease with distance because seismic energy spreads outward through space.
2. **Radiation pattern:** $R_i^P(\theta,\phi)$ The amplitude depends on observation direction relative to the fault geometry.
3. **Source time dependence:** $\dot{M}(t)$ Earthquakes occur over finite time rather than instantaneously. This term controls pulse duration, waveform shape, frequency content, and rupture duration effects.

👉 In this lecture we focus primarily on the source-time term.

---

## Static vs Dynamic Deformation

An earthquake permanently displaces the two sides of a fault and involves both static deformation and dynamic wave radiation.

Near the fault:
- deformation remains after the earthquake
- offsets may be visible in roads, fences, or GPS measurements

Far from the source:
- seismic waves pass by as transient disturbances
- the ground eventually returns to rest

Thus near-field deformation is permanent and far-field seismic radiation is temporary.

## Time-Dependent Seismic Moment

Previously we described earthquake size using the scalar seismic moment $M_0 = \mu D A$.  For a real earthquake, slip evolves with time, so the moment becomes $M(t)$. This function describes how fault slip accumulates during rupture.

A simple earthquake moment function might look like:

Key features:
- moment grows during rupture
- moment eventually reaches a final value
- the final offset corresponds to permanent fault displacement

Far-field body waves depend on $\dot{M}(t)$ rather than directly on $M(t)$.

Physically:

- constant fault slip does not radiate waves
- changing slip radiates seismic energy
- acceleration and deceleration of rupture generate seismic waves

## Source Time Functions

The function describing the temporal evolution of rupture is called the **Source Time Function**

The source time function controls:
- rupture duration
- pulse width
- dominant frequency content
- spectral shape

# Source Spectra and Corner Frequency

## Earthquake Pulses in the Frequency Domain

The far-field displacement pulse generated by an earthquake can also be described in the frequency domain.

A short-duration pulse in time corresponds to a broad spectrum in frequency.

The displacement spectrum of a simple earthquake source typically has the form:

- high frequencies → amplitudes decrease rapidly
- transition occurs near the **corner frequency** $f_c$

---

## Long-Period Spectral Level

At sufficiently low frequencies, the displacement spectrum approaches a constant value $\Omega_0$.

Physically:
- $\Omega_0$ is proportional to the total area beneath the displacement pulse
- larger earthquakes produce larger low-frequency amplitudes

👉 The long-period spectral level is proportional to the scalar seismic moment.

---

## Corner Frequency

The transition between low- and high-frequency behavior occurs near the:

> **Corner frequency**
$$
f_c
$$

The corner frequency is inversely related to rupture duration:

$$
f_c \propto \frac{1}{\tau}
$$

where:
- $\tau$ = characteristic pulse width or rupture duration

Interpretation:
- short ruptures → high corner frequency
- long ruptures → low corner frequency

Large earthquakes:
- rupture for longer times
- radiate more low-frequency energy
- therefore have lower corner frequencies

👉 Corner frequency contains information about rupture duration and source size.

---

## Displacement vs Velocity Spectra

Far-field displacement depends on:

$$
u(t)\propto \dot{M}(t)
$$

Taking a time derivative corresponds in the frequency domain to multiplication by frequency:

$$
\mathcal{F}\left[\frac{d}{dt}u(t)\right]
\propto
f\,U(f)
$$

Thus:
- velocity spectra are enhanced at high frequencies relative to displacement spectra
- acceleration spectra emphasize high frequencies even more strongly

This is why:
- broadband velocity records often appear much “sharper”
- displacement records emphasize long-period energy

👉 Differentiation boosts high-frequency energy.

---

## Estimating Seismic Moment from Spectra

For body waves, the low-frequency spectral level is related to scalar seismic moment by:

$$
M_0
=
4\pi \rho c^3 r
\frac{\Omega_0}{U_{\phi\theta}}
$$

where:
- $\rho$ = density
- $c$ = wave velocity
- $r$ = source-receiver distance
- $\Omega_0$ = low-frequency displacement spectral level
- $U_{\phi\theta}$ = radiation pattern term

So earthquake size can be estimated directly from seismic wave observations.

Low-frequency amplitudes are relatively stable because they are less affected by:
- attenuation
- scattering
- small-scale heterogeneity

As a result scalar moment estimates are often robust and moment magnitude is more reliable than high-frequency source measurements.

👉 Long-period seismic waves provide one of the best measures of earthquake size.

---

## Near-Field vs Far-Field Motion

Near-field displacement resembles the cumulative source motion $M(t)$. Far-field displacement resembles $\dot{M}(t)$.

Most seismometers record velocity $\dot{u}(t)$ which introduces another time derivative $\ddot{M}(t)$

Thus:
- displacement records resemble $\dot{M}(t)$
- velocity records resemble $\ddot{M}(t)$

# Finite Rupture and Directivity

## Point Sources vs Finite Faults

So far we have mostly treated earthquakes as point sources.  Large earthquakes, however, rupture over finite distance and time.

A simple idealized source is the **Haskell source** where seismic moment increases linearly with time.

```{figure} ../figures/10_haskell_source.png
---
name: Haskell Source
width: 400px
alt: Moment and moment rate functions for a Haskell source model.
---
A ramp function in seismic moment produces a boxcar far-field displacement pulse.
```

In this model:
- $M(t)$ is a ramp function
- $\dot{M}(t)$ is a boxcar pulse
- the pulse width is the **rise time** $\tau_r$

If rupture propagates along a fault of length $L$ with rupture velocity $v_r$:

$$
\tau_d = \frac{L}{v_r}
$$

then the observed pulse duration depends on station geometry because waves from different parts of the fault arrive at different times.

👉 Finite faults produce azimuth-dependent waveform durations.

---

# Rupture Directivity

```{figure} ../figures/10_directivity_geometry.png
---
width: 650px
---
A unilateral rupture propagating along a fault.
```

For a station at azimuth $\theta$ relative to rupture propagation:

$$
\tau_c(\theta)
=
\frac{L}{c}
\left(
1-\frac{v_r}{c}\cos\theta
\right)
$$

where:
- $L$ = rupture length
- $v_r$ = rupture velocity
- $c$ = seismic wave velocity

This effect is called **directivity**.

### Forward directivity
- rupture propagates toward the station
- shorter pulse duration
- larger amplitudes

### Backward directivity
- rupture propagates away from the station
- longer pulse duration
- smaller amplitudes

If slip at each point on the fault occurs as a ramp function, the observed pulse becomes trapezoidal:


👉 Directivity redistributes seismic energy in time and strongly influences observed shaking.
